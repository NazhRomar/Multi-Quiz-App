## Etymology of Technology
The word **technology** comes from two Greek words, transliterated **techne** and **logos**.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Techne** | Art, skill, craft, or the way, manner, or means by which a thing is gained |
| **Logos** | Word, the utterance by which inward thought is expressed, a saying, or an expression |
| **Technologia** (Greek form) | "Systematic treatment" of grammar or an art |
| **Technology** (early English use, 17th century) | A discourse or technical treatise on an art, or a systematic explanation of practical crafts |
| **Technology** (modern meaning, by the 19th century) | Expanded from the study of mechanical and industrial arts to mean the actual application of those arts and sciences |

## Key Definitions
- **Technology:** the making, modification, usage, and knowledge of tools, machines, techniques, crafts, systems, and methods of organization, in order to solve a problem or improve a pre-existing solution.
- **Entrepreneurship:** the practice of converting good ideas to something profitable.
  - Also: a process of identifying and starting a business venture, sourcing and organizing the required resources, and taking both the risks and rewards associated with the venture.
  - Also (businessdictionary.com): the capacity and willingness to develop, organize, and manage a business venture along with any of its risks in order to make a profit.
- **Technopreneurship:** simply entrepreneurship in a technology-intensive context. It is the process of merging technology and entrepreneurial talent and skills.
  - Also (technopreneurship360): the process of combining technological innovation with entrepreneurial skills to create new businesses or transform existing ones.

## Nature of Entrepreneurship

| Comparison | Difference |
|---|---|
| **Entrepreneurship vs Management** | The entrepreneur is the **owner** of the company and bears both profit and loss. Management **operates and runs** the company with the help of various administrative functions. |
| **Entrepreneur vs Investor** (stocks/bonds/another business) | An entrepreneur establishes the business through an idea and favorable propositions, launching a company to invent or innovate a good or service while accepting all the risks of failure. An investor approaches an entrepreneur to make money through investing, providing capital in exchange for the expectation of future gains and profits. |
| **Entrepreneurship vs Moonlighting** | Moonlighting is holding down two or more jobs at the same time. Usually one is the primary source of income and the other is a side gig or freelance work. Moonlighters want extra money, to pursue a passion outside their nine-to-five job, or to gain experience and build their resume. |
| **Entrepreneurship vs Technopreneurship** | An entrepreneur identifies an opportunity, converts it into a product or service, estimates earnings and profit, and builds a successful business with it. A technopreneur starts out with nothing but an "idea," defies existing practices and systems, and thinks of doing things differently. |

**Technopreneurship is a new breed of entrepreneurship.** It brings together people who are intelligent, driven, creative, tech-savvy, passionate, and have an appetite for calculated risk.

## Nature of Technopreneurship
- It is **not as much about the money** as about the affirmation that the "idea" is a worthy one and that it works.
- The idea needs to work for the **greater common good**. It doesn't need to be a global blockbuster.
- It is an **expensive exercise**: technopreneurs need to work without pay until their ideas become commercially viable.
- It is not often easy. They have to persevere, lead and motivate their team, search for people with the money, pitch their idea, and get them to join forces. Here their **past work experience and network** become extremely useful.
- More often than not, technopreneurship **begins with the customer experience and works backward** to arrive at the technology that provides it.
- A technopreneur constantly learns and improves, innovates, and disrupts the workings of people, organizations, and nations as a whole.

## Advantages of a Small Business
1. Uses low-level or intermediate technology rather than the high technology of large firms.
2. The individual worker gets to work on a whole or part of an operation.
3. Small-scale producers can use raw materials or by-products in limited volume, which would otherwise be disposed of as waste in large-scale factories.
4. Faster communication.
5. Can easily modify the product to address market demand and preferences.
6. In times of economic crisis, can easily adjust production, personnel, and other systems.

## Disadvantages of a Small Business
1. Lacks skills in marketing planning and doing market studies.
2. Hardly undertakes promotional activities, notably media advertising.
3. Must cope with wastage and late delivery of raw materials.
4. May not be able to immediately raise salaries and wages.
5. Lack of technical know-how in case of machine breakdown, quality control problems, and obsolete technology.
6. Many fail because of the lack of a workable and realistic financial plan.
7. Many entrepreneurs do not know how to generate funds.
8. Control and administration are centralized in the hands of an owner-manager.
9. Inadequate internal administration: poor and inadequate record keeping, unsystematic recruitment and selection of workers, and unscientific decision making.

## Role of the Entrepreneur/Technopreneur
Source: DTI.

1. **Plans, organizes, and puts together all the resources** needed to start a new enterprise and run it on a sustained basis.
   - **Human resources:** workers, managers, customers, and suppliers
   - **Non-human resources:** land and building, money, machines, materials, and methods or processes
2. **Takes risks**, not the way gamblers and speculators do, but by putting resources together to realize business objectives. It takes risk-taking to invest money, effort, and other resources into a new undertaking whose result is uncertain. They might win or lose, get rich or go bankrupt, but take chances anyway.
3. **Innovates.** Continuously creates and develops new products and services based on the needs of society, and looks for ways to produce them more economically.
4. **Helps build the nation.** Small entrepreneurs are often called the **"backbone of the economy"** or **"the engine of growth"** because they play these roles in society:

| Role | What it means |
|---|---|
| **4a. Bring about employment** | Businesses hire people. When business slows down, unemployment increases. Employed people feed their families, send children to school, provide decent homes, and pay income taxes. |
| **4b. Improve the quality of life** | New products and services make life easier and more comfortable. Example: cooking revolutionized by microwave ovens, turbo broilers, pressure cookers, and multipurpose cookers. |
| **4c. Contribute to a wider distribution of income** | Entrepreneurs find raw materials in rural areas. Locating in the provinces spreads development to neglected communities, which helps slow down migration and overcrowding in the metropolis. |
| **4d. Utilize resources for national productivity** | Putting natural resources to good use hastens economic development. Example: handicrafts from pandan, rattan, bamboo, anahaw, nipa, buri, even cogon grass. Bamboo is now used as computer casing, part of a green computing scheme. |
| **4e. Generate social benefits through government** | Taxes, duties, and licenses paid by entrepreneurs fund social services: roads and bridges, educational and medical services and facilities, and maintenance of peace and order. |

## Partnership
"Two heads can be better than one."

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Pooled financial resources | Unlimited liability |
| Shared responsibilities | Disagreements |
| Ease of formation | Difficulty in withdrawing from agreement |
| Tax advantages | Lack of continuity |

### Limited Partnerships
- **Limited Partnership:** includes at least one general partner and at least one limited partner. Limited partners have limited liability.
- **Limited Liability Partnership:** all partners are actively involved but have some form of limited liability. The amount of liability differs per state.

| General Partnership | Limited Partnership |
|---|---|
| All partners have the right to participate in management and share in any profits/losses | All partners contribute financially and share in profits, but the limited partner(s) **cannot actively participate in management** |

### Family Limited Partnerships
- Parents are **general partners**; children are **limited partners**.
- Parents transfer assets to the limited partners while still maintaining control. This strategy:
  - Reduces gift and inheritance taxes
  - Protects family assets from creditors and lawsuits
- But watch out for the IRS: Family Limited Partnerships can attract tax auditors.

## Corporation
"An artificial reality."
- A corporation is a **legal entity, separate and distinct from its owners**.
- Corporations are owned by **stockholders**.
- The **Board of Directors** establishes the mission and objectives.
- The Board is **elected by the stockholders** to represent their interests.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Limited liability | Expense/complexity of formation and operation |
| Permanence | Double taxation |
| Easy to transfer ownership | Paperwork and regulation |
| Ability to raise capital | Conflicts of interest |
| Specialized management | |

### Other Types of Corporations
"Same but different": S Corporation, Closed Corporation, Non-profit Corporation.

| Type | Key advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **S Corp.** | IRS does not tax earnings separately; stockholders have limited liability | No more than 100 stockholders; stockholders must be US citizens or permanent residents |
| **Statutory Close Corp.** | Not required to have a board or hold annual meetings; owners can participate in management while keeping limited liability | Limited number of stockholders; stockholders must offer shares to owners first before selling publicly; not all states allow this type |
| **Nonprofit Corp.** | Earnings exempt from federal and state income taxes; members/directors have limited liability; contributions by individuals are tax-deductible | May have dues-paying members but no stockholders; can't distribute dividends; can't make political donations; must keep accurate records to document tax exemption |

## Business Registration in the Philippines
Three types: Sole Proprietorship, Partnership, Corporation (reference: pnjgconsulting.com).

### Sole Proprietorship (registered with DTI)
**Requirements:**
- Create the business name to be registered in the DTI Business Name Registration System (BNRS)
- Applicant's TIN
- Copy of identification of citizenship (government-issued IDs: Driver's License, Voter's ID, Passport, etc.)
- Signed copy of undertaking from DTI BNRS
- Payment for application

**Process:**
1. Reserve, secure, and register the name from DTI
2. Present accomplished forms/documents for processing and evaluation
3. Get the certificate from DTI
4. Secure business registration from the local government of the chosen location (Barangay Certificate, Mayor's Permit & Licenses)
5. Register the business with the Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR)
6. If with employees, register with SSS, PhilHealth, and HDMF (Pag-IBIG)

### Partnership (registered with SEC)
**Requirements:**
- Create the business name to be registered in SEC
- Draft of the Articles of Partnership
- SEC Form F-105 (for foreigner partners)
- Payment for application

**Process:**
1. Reserve, secure, and register the name from SEC
2. Submit duly notarized Articles of Partnership
3. Present completed forms and needed documents to SEC
4. Provide the necessary requirement if one of the partners is a foreigner
5. Present certification of minimum paid-up capital in a trust account from a local bank
6. Get the certificate from SEC
7. Secure business registration from the local government (Barangay Certificate, Mayor's Permit & Licenses)
8. Register the business with BIR
9. If with employees, register with SSS, PhilHealth, and HDMF

### Corporation (registered with SEC)
**Requirements:**
- Create the business name to be registered in SEC
- Must have at least 5 to 15 shareholders (as written on the slide)
- Draft of the articles of partnership and by-laws (as written on the slide)
- SEC Form F-100 (for corporations with more than 40%; the slide's text is cut off here)
- Payment for application
- Registration of paid-up capital and affidavit from the Corporate Treasurer
  - **Filipino corporation:** at least ₱100,000 paid-up capital
  - **Foreign-owned:** minimum paid-up capital is expected to be higher, depending on the type of corporation

**Process:**
1. Reserve, secure, and register the name from SEC
2. Provide the necessary requirement if one of the incorporators is a foreigner
3. Submit completed forms and documents to SEC: Cover Sheet, Name Verification Slip, Articles of Incorporation, Treasurer's Affidavit, By-Laws, Undertaking to change name
4. Get the certificate from SEC
5. Secure business registration from the local government (Barangay Certificate, Mayor's Permit & Licenses)
6. Register the business with BIR
7. If with employees, register with SSS, PhilHealth, and HDMF

| Form | Registers name with | Key document |
|---|---|---|
| Sole Proprietorship | DTI (BNRS) | DTI certificate |
| Partnership | SEC | Articles of Partnership |
| Corporation | SEC | Articles of Incorporation and By-Laws |

## Clear Performance Metrics (KPIs)
A successful incubation program tracks clear **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)** to monitor its ongoing **return on investment (ROI)**.

| Metric | Definition | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| **Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR)** | The predictable, total revenue a startup generates monthly from all active subscriptions | Total Number of Active Customers × Average Revenue Per User (ARPU) |
| **Annual Run Rate (ARR)** | An annualized projection of current recurring revenue, assuming constant customer volume and pricing | MRR × 12 |
| **Gross Margin** | The proportion of revenue remaining after deducting the direct costs of producing and delivering goods or services (COGS) | [(Total Revenue − COGS) ÷ Total Revenue] × 100 |
| **Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)** | The total sales and marketing spending required to acquire a single new customer during a specified period | Total Sales & Marketing Expenses ÷ New Customers Acquired |
| **Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)** | The total projected gross profit a business expects from an individual customer account over the entire relationship | ARPU × Customer Lifetime (months) × Gross Margin % |
| **LTV to CAC Ratio** | Compares the total value derived from a customer against the cost of acquiring them | LTV ÷ CAC |

> **Key line:** The industry benchmark for LTV to CAC is a target ratio of **3:1 or higher**. A **1:1** ratio means revenue is entirely depleted by acquisition costs.

## ARPU and the BayadPo Scenario
**ARPU (Average Revenue Per User, or Average Revenue Per Unit):** a fundamental unit economics metric for evaluating platform revenue per active customer.

**Standard formula:** ARPU = Total Revenue ÷ Total Active Users

**Scenario:** BayadPo is a homegrown tech startup. Its mobile app lets Sari-Sari stores across Luzon, Visayas, and Mindanao order digital inventory, accept QR payments, and disburse micro-loans to community members. The finance team must calculate August's monthly ARPU for its institutional investors.

| August operational metrics | Value |
|---|---|
| Time period | August 1 to August 31 (1 month) |
| Total revenue earned | ₱12,500,000 |
| Monthly Active Users (MAU) | 50,000 active store owners |
| **Monthly ARPU** | ₱12,500,000 ÷ 50,000 = **₱250 / user / month** |

### Why This ₱250 ARPU Is Actionable
- **Validating the Mass-Market Strategy:** in the Philippines, tech platforms target sheer volume over high individual prices. Each store brings in a modest amount, but aggregate scale generates massive velocity (₱12.5 million/month).
- **Hard Marketing Limits:** if marketing runs Facebook ads or referral incentives (e.g., "Get ₱50 free load when you sign up a neighbor"), they now know their CAC framework. Spending **₱300 to acquire one store is dangerous** if it only returns ₱250 gross revenue monthly. The user must stick around for months just to pay off the ad spend.
- **Cross-Selling Push:** to lift ARPU from ₱250 to ₱350, management doesn't need millions of new users immediately. Rolling out new features, like integrated micro-insurance or bill payments, lets current users spend an extra ₱100/month inside the system.

## Innovation: Core Pillars
To qualify as a genuine innovation, an idea must satisfy **three** criteria:

| Pillar | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Novelty** | The concept must be novel, or applied in a substantially innovative manner within its target context. |
| **Implementation** | The idea must go beyond theoretical concepts, patents, or preliminary designs, advancing to full development, official launch, and practical application. |
| **Value Creation** | The initiative must generate demonstrable benefits: better operational efficiency, lower production costs, higher organizational revenue, or the advancement of human well-being. |

## Innovation: Types

### 1. By Business Dimension

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Product/Service Innovation** | Introducing a completely new or significantly improved physical good or service to the market | Tesla creating electric vehicles with autonomous driving |
| **Process Innovation** | Changing how a product is made, delivered, or supported to increase efficiency, reduce costs, or improve quality | Amazon using automated Kiva robots in fulfillment centers to pick and pack orders faster |
| **Business Model Innovation** | Re-engineering how a company creates, delivers, and captures value (changing pricing structure or target demographic) | Spotify shifting music from buying individual albums to an all-you-can-listen monthly subscription |

### 2. By Market Impact (Clayton Christensen)
Coined by **Clayton Christensen**, looking at how innovation alters the competitive landscape.

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Sustaining Innovation** | Caters to high-end customers in existing markets by making good products better. Strictly follows the established rules of the industry. | A razor company adding a 5th blade or a lubricating strip to an existing razor line |
| **Disruptive Innovation** | Introduces a simpler, more affordable, or more convenient alternative. Starts with low-end or unserved customers before moving upmarket and displacing industry leaders. | Digital photography apps and smartphone cameras eliminating consumer film cameras (Kodak) |

### 3. By Tech vs. Market Nuance (Henderson-Clark Model)
Examines how innovation impacts **core components** versus the **overarching system architecture**.

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Incremental Innovation** | Minor modifications to existing components while keeping the established architecture intact | Integrating a faster microchip into an existing smartphone model |
| **Modular Innovation** | Complete replacement of a core component while preserving the overall system structure | Replacing a gas engine with an electric motor inside an existing car frame |
| **Architectural Innovation** | Reconfigures linkages and relationships among unchanged individual components | Reorganizing standard computing hardware into a cloud server architecture |
| **Radical Innovation** | Comprehensive overhaul of both core components and architectural connections | The shift from mechanical typewriters to digital word processing systems |

## New Products Classification (Thomas Robertson)
The **Consumer's Viewpoint**, proposed by **Thomas Robertson**.

| Type | Level | Definition | Customer impact | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Continuous Innovation** | 1. Most common | A modified or upgraded product rather than a totally new one. No change in consumer habits and no new learning curve. Same function as its predecessor, with improved efficiency or features. | **Zero Behavioral Friction:** consumers use it seamlessly, exactly as before | A new soft drink flavor; the iPhone 17 Pro replacing the iPhone 16 Pro; a laundry detergent adding a "lemon fresh" scent |
| **Dynamically Continuous Innovation** | 2. Moderate disruption | A significantly new product, or a major overhaul of an existing one. The core purpose stays familiar, but the technology, method, or design forces users to slightly adapt their behavior. | **Low-to-Moderate Learning Curve:** users adapt to a new mechanism to reach a familiar goal | Manual toothbrushes to electric sonic toothbrushes; keys to a smart fingerprint door lock; cable TV to streaming apps |
| **Discontinuous Innovation** | 3. Rarest and high impact | An entirely unprecedented product type that meets a need in a way never done before. Creates severe disruption and completely overhauls consumer behavior. Requires extensive customer education. | **Massive Learning Curve:** completely alters daily routines, lifestyles, and entire industries | The automobile (replacing horses); the personal computer (replacing typewriters); the birth of commercial internet browsers |

## Intellectual Property (IP) in Tech

| Type | Protects | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Patents** | Functional inventions, proprietary hardware designs, novel mechanical processes, or specific underlying software architectures that produce a tangible technical result | Software code itself is generally **not patentable** in most jurisdictions. Patents protect the system architecture, logic flow, or data processing method instead. |
| **Copyrights** | The **expression** of an idea, not the idea itself | The primary legal mechanism protecting your software source code, UI visuals, marketing content, and internal documentation |
| **Trademarks** | Brand identity, logos, business names, and distinct phrases | Crucial for safeguarding your app name, platform branding, and marketplace identity against duplicate copycats |
| **Trade Secrets** | Confidential business information that gives a competitive edge (e.g., source code repositories, mathematical algorithm weights, unpatented backend processes, customer databases) | Protected through operational cybersecurity and mandatory legal contracts (NDAs) |

### The Startup Strategy Rule of Thumb
- **Standard applications** (e.g., a regular e-commerce delivery app or basic social platform): focus on safeguarding the user interface, content, and brand presence. **Copyright and Trademarks** are your primary tools to secure code, custom visuals, and marketplace identity without excessive legal overhead.
- **Complex & breakthrough tech:** only explore the long, expensive path of **patent filing** if your software contains highly complex, breakthrough processing mechanics. It is reserved strictly for **deep-tech** innovations, such as advanced AI model architectures or hardware integrations.

## Crucial IP Risks for Tech Startups

| Risk | What happens |
|---|---|
| **The "First-to-File" Rule** | In the Philippines and most international jurisdictions, IP rights (especially patents and trademarks) go to whoever **files first, not who invented first**. Delaying registration leaves room for competitors to claim your architecture. |
| **Public Disclosure Traps** | Showcasing your prototype at a hackathon, pitch competition, or public demo day **before** filing a patent can be costly. Premature exposure can permanently destroy the "novelty" requirement for a patent. |
| **Third-Party Code Liability** | Building software heavily on open-source libraries without checking their licenses (like GPL or MIT) carries hidden risk. It can accidentally force your entire proprietary software suite to become open-source and free to the public. |

|---|---|---|---|
| **Waymo vs. Uber & Anthony Levandowski** ("The Stolen Blueprint") | Extreme risk of **trade secret theft during employee transitions** | Levandowski, a star engineer at Google's Waymo, downloaded **14,000+ confidential design files (10 GB)** before resigning. | **JGC Philippines, Inc. vs. Santiago DJ. Sillano** (The Software Lockout Case) | **Employee IP assignment and code locks** | Senior Engineer Sillano built custom software during employment. | **NBI-Microsoft Corporation vs. Beltron Computer** (G.R. ## IPR and Capstone Projects
**CHED Guidelines & IPOPHL context:** capstone projects are academic requirements, but proactive IPOPHL filing depends on commercialization plans.

| | 1. Documentation & Code (Copyright) | 2. Functional Devices (Patents) |
|---|---|---|
| **Protection status** | **Automatic** upon creation and storage (the moment your group writes the paper or saves code to a drive) | Requires formal **patent / utility model filing** |
| **Filing need** | No certificate needed to prove authorship | The underlying function is not automatically protected |
| **Covered assets** | Capstone paper, documentation, and source code | Hardware prototypes, physical devices, and system logic |
| **Vulnerability** | | High risk of market replication if disclosed early |

### When Filing for IPR Becomes Necessary
Shift from an "academic mindset" to an **"IPR filing mindset"** when your project meets these triggers:

| Trigger | Details |
|---|---|
| **1. Startup Ambitions** | If commercializing after graduation, file **before** any public showcase. The Philippines follows a strict "First-to-File" system, and a public defense can lose your rights. **Action:** file the patent before the public defense. |
| **2. Public Disclosure** | Presenting at innovation fairs, institutional repositories, or open hackathons counts as public disclosure and **permanently destroys patent novelty**. **Prevention:** review IP status before external events. |
| **3. University Policy** | Projects using major campus labs require IP review through the university's **ITSO**. **Incentive:** schools may sponsor (cover) the official filing fees for high-value tech. |
